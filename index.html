A full guide to this simulation

This page exists for one idea: probability is not created or destroyed. If more probability shows up in a region of space, it must have flowed in from next door. The continuity equation is that bookkeeping rule written in calculus.

1. What the wave packet is

The simulation shows a free Gaussian wave packet: a bump of probability that travels to the right (and slowly spreads). Units are chosen so ℏ = 1 and m = 1. That is only a choice of units — it does not change the meaning of the continuity equation.

The complex wave function Ψ(x,t) is the object that obeys Schrödinger’s equation. You never need its phase to understand continuity. What matters for probability accounting is the density

ρ(x,t) = |Ψ(x,t)|².

Interpret ρ(x,t) dx as the probability of finding the particle between x and x+dx at time t. The top plot draws that density as a function of x.

2. The window [a,b] and the total probability inside it

The green band is a fixed interval of space. The number

P[a,b](t) = ∫ab ρ(x,t) dx

is the probability that a position measurement at time t finds the particle somewhere inside that interval. In the readout this is simply called P.

From the simulation now

P = —. If this is near 0, the packet is mostly outside the window. If it is near 1, almost all of the probability is currently between a and b.

As the packet slides through the window, P rises and then falls. The whole point of continuity is to explain that rise and fall without inventing probability out of nothing.

3. What ∂ρ/∂t means

The symbol ∂ρ/∂t is the rate at which the density at a fixed location is changing. Hold your finger on one value of x and ask: is probability accumulating there, or draining away?

  • ∂ρ/∂t > 0: density at that point is increasing (filling).
  • ∂ρ/∂t < 0: density at that point is decreasing (emptying).
  • ∂ρ/∂t = 0: density there is momentarily steady.

Units: probability per unit length, per unit time. In the local-check plot, the solid blue curve is this quantity as a function of x.

For the whole window we integrate:

∫ab (∂ρ/∂t) dx = d/dt ∫ab ρ dx = dP/dt.

So the first big green-bordered number in “Balance for the window” is literally “how fast is the total probability inside [a,b] changing?”

∫ab (∂ρ/∂t) dx = —. A positive value means probability inside the window is increasing right now (the packet is entering, or still piling into the interval). A negative value means probability is leaving the window.

4. What the probability current J means

Density alone does not tell you which way probability is moving. Two packets can have the same bump of ρ while one travels right and the other left. The probability current J(x,t) supplies the missing flow information.

In one dimension, think of J(x,t) as the rate at which probability crosses the point x, from left to right, at time t:

  • J > 0: net flow to the right.
  • J < 0: net flow to the left.
  • |J| large: a lot of probability is crossing that point per unit time.

For this simulation (with ℏ = m = 1),

J = Im(Ψ* ∂Ψ/∂x).

You do not need to recompute that formula by hand while using the page — the middle plot already graphs J(x). The orange arrows at a and b on the density plot are the same quantity evaluated only at the two endpoints.

From the simulation now

J(a) = —, J(b) = —. If J(a) is larger than J(b), more probability is flowing in at the left end than out at the right end, so the window is gaining probability.

A helpful classical analogy: cars on a one-lane road. The number of cars per mile is like ρ. The number of cars per minute passing a mile marker is like J. Continuity is the statement that you cannot change the number of cars in a stretch of road except by cars entering or leaving through the ends.

5. What ∂J/∂x means (this is the subtle one)

The current J(x) can be different at neighboring points. The derivative ∂J/∂x measures that spatial change:

∂J/∂x ≈ [J(x+Δx) − J(x)] / Δx.

It answers: as I move a little to the right, does the rightward flow get stronger or weaker?

  • ∂J/∂x > 0: current is increasing as you move right. More probability is leaving the small neighborhood to the right than is entering from the left — a local divergence / emptying due to flow.
  • ∂J/∂x < 0: current is decreasing as you move right. More probability is entering from the left than leaving to the right — a local convergence / pile-up due to flow.

So ∂J/∂x is not “the current itself.” It is the imbalance of current across a tiny interval. That imbalance is exactly what forces the density at that spot to change.

Numerical example you can read off the probe

Quantity at x* Live value Meaning
(∂ρ/∂t)|x* — How fast density is changing at the probe.
(∂J/∂x)|x* — How fast the current is changing across the probe.
(∂ρ/∂t + ∂J/∂x)|x* — Must be ≈ 0 if continuity is true.

Concrete reading: suppose you see (∂ρ/∂t)|x* ≈ −0.12 and (∂J/∂x)|x* ≈ +0.12. Then density at the probe is falling at rate 0.12, and the current is increasing through that point at the same rate. Probability is not vanishing; it is being carried away because outflow on the right exceeds inflow on the left. Their sum is zero within numerical error.

Tiny-interval story. Take a short segment [x, x+Δx]. Probability enters at rate J(x) and leaves at rate J(x+Δx). Net gain per unit time is J(x) − J(x+Δx) ≈ −(∂J/∂x) Δx. Dividing by the length Δx gives the density’s rate of change: ∂ρ/∂t ≈ −∂J/∂x. That is the continuity equation.

6. Putting it together: ∂ρ/∂t + ∂J/∂x = 0

Rewrite the same relation as

∂ρ/∂t = −∂J/∂x.

Left side: how density is changing in time at a fixed place.
Right side: minus the spatial divergence of the current.
Continuity says these are the same number, always.

The bottom plot makes this visual: the solid blue curve is ∂ρ/∂t; the dashed orange curve is −∂J/∂x. If they lie on top of each other, the equation is holding across the whole line.

7. The window version (why the balance panel looks the way it does)

Integrate the local equation from a to b:

∫ab (∂ρ/∂t) dx + ∫ab (∂J/∂x) dx = 0.

The second integral collapses by the fundamental theorem of calculus:

∫ab (∂J/∂x) dx = J(b) − J(a).

Therefore

dP/dt = J(a) − J(b).

In words: the only way for probability inside the window to change is net flow through the ends. The balance panel shows both ingredients of the integrated continuity equation and then their sum.

9. What each plot is teaching

  1. Density plot. Where the particle is likely to be. The green band is the accounting region. Orange arrows show endpoint currents. The purple line is your chosen probe.
  2. Current plot. Direction and strength of probability flow. For a right-moving packet, J is typically a positive bump under the packet.
  3. Local check plot. Direct visual of continuity: ∂ρ/∂t (blue) should match −∂J/∂x (dashed orange). Where the packet’s leading edge is arriving, density rises and −∂J/∂x is positive; at the trailing edge, density falls.

10. A short study sequence

  1. Reset. Set a = -1, b = 1, probe at 0.
  2. Pause near t ≈ 1.5, when the packet overlaps the window.
  3. Read ∫(∂ρ/∂t) dx and ∫(∂J/∂x) dx. Confirm the sum is ~0.
  4. Slide x* across the packet. Watch ∂ρ/∂t flip from positive (front) to negative (back), while ∂J/∂x flips the opposite way so the local sum stays ~0.
  5. Increase k₀ and repeat: currents get larger, the same logic remains.

11. Common confusions

  • “Is J the same as velocity?” No. Velocity would be for a particle trajectory. J is a probability flux. For a plane wave of amplitude A one does have J = v|A|², which is why people say “speed times density,” but in general J is computed from Ψ as above.
  • “Why is the window sum using J(b)-J(a) while the verbal slogan says J(a)-J(b)?” Both appear. Continuity integrated gives dP/dt = J(a)-J(b) (influx minus outflux). Equivalently, dP/dt + [J(b)-J(a)] = 0. The balance panel shows the second form so you literally add the two integrals to see zero.
  • “The sum is not exactly machine zero.” The page uses finite differences in x and t. Residuals like 10−4 or 10−5 are numerical, not physical violations.
  • “Does this depend on the potential?” The continuity equation still holds when a real potential is present (as in the step potential lesson). This simulation uses a free packet only to keep the picture clean.

12. The one-sentence takeaway

∂ρ/∂t says how probability density is changing in time; ∂J/∂x says how the flow is strengthening or weakening across space; continuity says those two effects are exact opposites, so probability is only rearranged by flow — never created or destroyed.
